Technology

The platform

Universal Cancer Treatment Platform

We are developing a novel cancer immunotherapy platform, powered by artificial intelligence, to create different off-the-shelf injections for many types of cancer.

Our unique lipid nanoparticle mRNA (LNP-mRNA) technology aims to disguise cancer cells to look like measles and other common diseases and “trick” the body into killing them.

We have developed our lead preclinical candidate. In independent lab tests, human antiviral T cells killed nearly 100% of CancerVax-treated human liver cancer cells in culture.

Two approaches

Personalized vs. Off-the-Shelf

Personalized cancer vaccines, like those being developed by Moderna and Merck and by BioNTech, are made for one patient at a time. They’re designed to teach the immune system to recognize features unique to that patient’s tumor.

We start from the other direction. We’re developing off-the-shelf treatments for specific groups of cancers, not specific patients.

The problem

Why cancer can escape immune detection

The immune system can recognize a virus it has seen before. Cancer is harder to spot because it starts in the body’s own cells. A tumor can grow without triggering a response strong enough to stop it.

Think of measles. After infection or vaccination, your immune system remembers the virus and can fight it off again in the future.

Cytomegalovirus (CMV), another common virus, shows how large that response can be. In some older adults with prior CMV infection, up to 25% of CD8 T cells in the blood can recognize CMV.

We aim to put that immune memory to work against cancer, which could give the immune system a powerful head start.

The solution

A Revolutionary Cancer Treatment

Put the body’s virus fighters to work against cancer. Our lead preclinical candidate is designed to make cancer cells display multiple familiar viral signals so existing antiviral T cells can recognize and attack them.

Immune memory

Potential long-term cancer immunity

The first attack starts when antiviral T cells recognize familiar viral signals. As cancer cells die, they can reveal unique features of the cancer itself. That gives the immune system a chance to take a “snapshot” of the cancer, not just the disguise.

When the immune system learns to recognize those additional targets, it’s called epitope spreading. In our preclinical studies, we’re evaluating whether the initial response can broaden in this way and build lasting immune memory against cancer recurrence.

Delivery and selectivity

Limiting effects on healthy cells

After an IV infusion, lipid nanoparticles can build up in the liver and increase the risk of liver toxicity.

Our lipid nanoparticles are designed to deliver Smart mRNA to target cancer cells while limiting buildup in the liver. In a mouse study, our Marker1-targeted nanoparticles showed less liver buildup than the conventional formulation we tested.

Smart mRNA adds a second safeguard. It’s designed to limit activity in healthy cells while prompting target cancer cells to display multiple familiar viral signals.

The tumor microenvironment

Turning “cold” tumors “hot”

Cold tumors have little immune activity inside. Killer T cells may gather at the edges without getting in. Hot tumors typically have more active killer T cells inside.

Checkpoint inhibitors like Keytruda® help take the brakes off T cells. But that may not be enough when few T cells get inside a cold tumor. What if we could turn that tumor into a magnet for T cells?

We start inside the cancer cell. Smart mRNA is designed to make it display multiple viral signals the body’s antiviral T cells already recognize. Our goal is to put those T cells to work inside the tumor and turn it from cold to hot.

Biomarker discovery

Powered by artificial intelligence

We use proprietary AI to search public cancer datasets for markers shared across groups of cancers. Marker1 guides the nanoparticle to target cells. Marker2, built into Smart mRNA, is designed to activate the treatment selectively in cancer cells.
